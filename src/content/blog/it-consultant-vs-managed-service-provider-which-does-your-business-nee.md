Managed service providers want you on a monthly retainer. That's the business model. They'll monitor your network, manage your backups, handle your help desk tickets, patch your servers, and generally become the IT department you don't have in-house.

For some businesses, this makes perfect sense. For most, it's expensive insurance against problems that either don't exist yet or could be solved once and then mostly forgotten about.

The question isn't "which is better?" It's "what problem are you actually trying to solve?"

## When an MSP is the right answer

You need managed services if your business genuinely requires ongoing IT support. Not occasional support. Ongoing.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

- You have 20+ employees with laptops, email accounts, and file shares that break in unpredictable ways
- Your software stack includes on-premises infrastructure that needs active maintenance
- You have compliance requirements (HIPAA, SOC 2, PCI) that demand continuous monitoring and documentation
- You can't afford even a few hours of downtime without serious revenue impact
- Your people need someone to call when Outlook stops syncing at 3 PM on a Tuesday

If three or more of those are true, an MSP is probably worth the money. The monthly fee buys you predictable response times and the peace of mind that comes from knowing someone else is responsible when things break.

But if your IT environment is mostly SaaS apps, a handful of employees, and infrastructure that works fine until it doesn't — you're probably paying for a service you use twice a year.

## What a consultant actually does (and doesn't do)

A consultant shows up for a specific thing, does it, writes down what they did, and then leaves.

The specific thing might be:

- Reviewing your backup strategy and fixing the gaps
- Migrating you off a server you're still running in a closet somewhere
- Evaluating whether your security setup is reasonable for your risk profile
- Helping you make a [vendor decision](/services/it-consulting/) without getting sold something you don't need

What a consultant doesn't do: monitor your systems, take calls about password resets, or maintain an ongoing ticketing relationship. If something breaks six months after the engagement ends, you're calling someone else or calling them back as a new project.

This is the right fit if most of your technology works most of the time, but you have a specific question or gap you need senior-level help closing.

## The hybrid case that tricks people

Some MSPs will pitch a "hybrid" model: light monitoring, plus project work when you need it, all under one monthly agreement.

This sounds reasonable. It's usually not.

What happens is you end up paying a baseline fee for monitoring you don't really need, and then paying project rates on top of that when you actually want something done. The monitoring rarely catches anything you wouldn't have noticed yourself, and the project work gets slotted into the queue behind the MSP's higher-paying full-service clients.

You're better off either committing to a real managed services relationship (which costs real money but delivers real value) or calling in a consultant when you have a concrete problem to solve.

## The actual decision tree

Start here: **do you have someone internally who can reset passwords, troubleshoot printer issues, and field basic IT questions?**

If yes, you probably don't need an MSP. You need occasional outside help on things that person can't do — infrastructure projects, security reviews, [disaster recovery planning](/services/it-consulting/), vendor evaluations. Call a consultant.

If no, and you can't hire someone, an MSP might be the right answer. But get specific about the SLA. "Business hours support" is very different from "24/7 uptime monitoring," and you're going to pay a lot more for the second one.

## What to ask before you sign anything

Whether you're talking to an MSP or a consultant, ask these three questions:

1. **What exactly am I getting for the monthly fee?** If the answer is vague ("proactive monitoring and support"), push for specifics. Monitored how often? Support within what timeframe?

2. **What happens when I need something outside the base agreement?** MSPs charge project rates on top of the retainer. Make sure you know what those are.

3. **How do I get out if this doesn't work?** A 30-day cancellation clause is standard. Anything longer is a red flag.

Most small businesses don't need someone monitoring their network 24/7. They need someone competent to show up when there's a real problem, fix it correctly, and document it so it doesn't happen again. That's what a consultant does. An MSP is for when "someone competent showing up later" isn't fast enough.
